## I. Introduction

The role of AI in education has transformed learning by providing personalized tutoring, instant feedback, and access to vast knowledge. In Software Engineering, AI is especially relevant because it accelerates code generation, debugging, design patterns, and testing, significantly boosting productivity. However, it can also become a crutch, offering instant answers when students would otherwise spend hours building deeper understanding.

Generally, and in ICS 314, I used AI tools such as large language models for explanations, code completion, and prompt engineering. I began the semester primarily with Grok for conceptual understanding and switched to GitHub Copilot for its seamless integration with VS Code, which made it far more practical for day-to-day development work.

## II. Personal Experience with AI

### Experience WODs (e.g., E18)

I used minimal AI for Experience WODs. The tutorial videos were significantly more effective for my learning, so I relied on them heavily. The only notable exception was E51: Digits, where my contact cards displayed vertically instead of horizontally despite following the tutorial exactly. I used Copilot with the prompt: “Why are my Bootstrap cards stacking vertically when they should be horizontal? Here is my code: [pasted code].” It quickly identified the missing flexbox or grid properties. This was useful for unblocking me, but I still had to manually understand and apply the CSS concepts afterward.

### In-class Practice WODs

I used AI more frequently during in-class practice WODs, but always as a supplement rather than a replacement. I made a conscious effort to understand the material myself and turned to Copilot or Grok only when stuck. It helped me move forward productively while reinforcing my learning.

### In-class WODs

I relied on AI heavily for graded in-class WODs. I would often paste the entire problem description into Grok or Copilot. Early in the semester the solutions frequently contained errors that I had to debug myself. By the end of the semester, it became noticeably easier to get working solutions—likely due to both better prompting on my part and the tools improving. I avoided AI only on Next.js template WODs, which felt straightforward enough to complete independently.

While AI helped me finish under time pressure, I disliked the all-or-nothing grading because it sometimes rewarded effective prompting more than genuine understanding. The cost was shallower learning on those problems.

### Essays

AI played a significant role in my essay writing, including this one. My process was consistent: I wrote the full first draft myself in Google Docs, focusing only on content. I then fed it to AI with prompts such as “Improve clarity, fix grammar, and enhance flow while preserving my original ideas,” followed by “Convert this to clean Markdown with proper headings and lists.” I reviewed every suggestion and made final decisions myself. This approach was extremely helpful for polishing and formatting, especially early in the semester when I was still getting comfortable with Markdown.

### Final Project

For the final project I switched almost entirely to GitHub Copilot. Built with Next.js, TypeScript, React, Prisma, PostgreSQL, Vercel, and GitHub, the project was complex, and I used Copilot constantly for nearly every major feature—database schema, authentication, review system, course data management, and deployment. Typical prompts included “Generate a Prisma schema for users, courses, and reviews with proper relations” or “Help debug this Vercel deployment error.”

Copilot became markedly better toward the end of the semester, excelling at repetitive tasks and standard patterns. The benefit was massive time savings and faster iteration. The cost was that I still needed to maintain strong oversight for architecture and integration decisions.

### Learning a Concept / Tutorial

AI became an essential learning companion. When struggling with React hooks, TypeScript types, or Prisma schemas, I would ask Copilot or ChatGPT to break down concepts or walk through tutorials. Prompts like “Explain React useEffect with dependencies and show a practical example in a Next.js app” made dense documentation far more accessible. This approach reduced frustration and helped me grasp material more quickly and confidently.

### Answering a Question in Class or in Discord

I did not frequently answer others’ questions, but I regularly used AI to prepare my own. When unsure about a concept, I would query Copilot or Grok to clarify and formulate a solid response. This made me more efficient and independent when contributing to discussions.

### Asking or Answering a Smart Question

Toward the end of the semester, when I ran into deployment issues with my individual project on Vercel, I chose to ask classmates directly rather than using AI. Their practical, context-specific advice proved more valuable than what AI could offer at that moment and reinforced the importance of peer collaboration.

### Coding Example (e.g., “give an example of using Underscore .pluck”)

AI was extremely helpful here. Instead of digging through documentation, I could simply ask “Give me a clear example of using Underscore.js _.pluck on an array of objects.” I received immediate, usable snippets that accelerated learning and application.

### Explaining Code

When I encountered confusing code, I would paste it into AI with prompts like “Explain this code line by line in plain language.” This was one of the most consistently useful applications of AI, helping me debug faster and truly understand how different parts of a program worked together.

### Writing Code

My use of AI for writing code evolved during the semester. Early on I used Grok for starter code and guidance. Later I relied heavily on Copilot’s real-time suggestions in VS Code. I would begin a component and let it complete patterns, especially for repetitive tasks. I always reviewed and adjusted the output to fit project requirements. Overall, it reduced overwhelm and let me focus more on problem-solving than syntax.

### Documenting Code

To be honest, I didn’t spend much time documenting my code this semester. Most of my focus was on getting features working and fixing bugs, so I didn’t add many comments or write detailed documentation. Looking back, I realize that better documentation could have made the code easier to understand for myself and my teammates. It’s something I’d like to improve on in future projects.

### Quality Assurance (e.g., “What’s wrong with this code”)

AI was very effective for QA. Prompts such as “What’s wrong with this code and how do I fix it?” or “Fix the ESLint errors in this snippet” quickly identified bugs and style issues. It helped me keep code clean and taught me to recognize common mistakes.

### Other Uses in ICS 314 Not Listed

I frequently used AI to summarize and extract key information from lengthy official documentation (Bootstrap, React, Prisma, Postgres). Prompts like “Summarize how to query related data in Prisma using include” saved me hours of reading and helped me get started with new technologies much faster.

## III. Impact on Learning and Understanding

Incorporating AI into my learning process has made a noticeable difference in how I approach software engineering. AI tools helped me grasp complex concepts faster by providing instant explanations, code samples, and troubleshooting tips. This not only improved my comprehension but also boosted my confidence in tackling new technologies. I found that using AI encouraged me to experiment more and develop my problem-solving skills, since I could quickly test ideas and get feedback. However, I also noticed that relying too much on AI sometimes made me less likely to dig deep into documentation or fully understand the “why” behind certain solutions.

## IV. Practical Applications

Outside of ICS 314, I haven’t really used AI for other projects or events yet. Most of my experience with AI so far has been within the context of this course, where I used it to help with assignments and the final project. I haven’t participated in hackathons or applied AI tools to real-world software engineering challenges outside of class, but I can see how it could be useful for speeding up development and brainstorming solutions in the future.

## V. Challenges and Opportunities

One challenge I faced was that AI-generated code wasn’t always a perfect fit for my project’s structure or requirements, so I had to be careful not to accept suggestions blindly. Sometimes, AI would miss context or make assumptions that didn’t apply. On the other hand, there’s a huge opportunity to use AI as a learning companion—helping students get unstuck, explore new ideas, and reinforce best practices. I think more structured integration of AI into coursework could help students use it more effectively and responsibly.

## VI. Comparative Analysis

Compared to traditional teaching methods, AI-enhanced learning is much more interactive and immediate. Instead of waiting for office hours or searching through forums, I could get answers and examples right away. This made learning more engaging and helped with knowledge retention, since I could apply concepts directly to my code. However, traditional methods still have value, especially for building foundational understanding and encouraging deeper exploration. The best results came from combining both approaches.

## VII. Future Considerations

Looking ahead, I think AI will play an even bigger role in software engineering education. As AI tools become more advanced, they could offer personalized learning paths, real-time feedback, and smarter code reviews. The main challenge will be making sure students use AI to enhance their understanding, not just as a shortcut. Educators might need to develop guidelines and assignments that encourage thoughtful, critical use of AI.

## VIII. Conclusion

Overall, AI has been a valuable addition to my software engineering education. It made learning faster, more flexible, and less intimidating, especially when tackling new or difficult topics. To get the most out of AI, I recommend integrating it intentionally into courses, teaching students how to use it wisely, and balancing it with traditional learning methods. This way, students can build both strong technical skills and a deeper understanding of software engineering principles.
